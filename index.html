The cashout button has a meter running

Every gig app ships an instant cashout button next to the balance, and the fee is printed right on it: fifty cents a tap on most platforms, 1.5 percent where the cut is proportional. A driver who cashes out every day runs the meter twenty taps a month. At fifty cents that is ten dollars a month, $120 a year. At 1.5 percent on $800 of monthly earnings, the same daily habit is $12 a month, $144 a year, 1.5 percent of gross. The patience price is zero, and it arrives on the same day every week anyway.

Flat-fee apps and percent apps bill patience on different schedules, and the cross sits near $670 a month of earnings. Below it the percent fee is the cheaper button: at $400 a month the daily habit runs $6, $72 a year, while the flat meter bills $10, 2.5 percent of gross. Above it the flat fee wins: at $800 the percent habit costs $12 a month against the same $10, $144 against $120 a year. The flat structure taxes the smaller balance harder per dollar earned, the regressive direction, and the daily habit compounds it either way.

A $35 overdraft covered by an emergency fifty-cent tap costs $35.50 and buys the day; that is the one tap the fee earns. The alternative costs nothing but patience: the weekly payout lands, the balance never sits in limbo for the meter to bill it. The discipline version prices each tap before pressing: flat-fee apps are a per-tap tax, percent apps bill the whole month's gross for the same button. Four planned taps a year, one per quarter at fifty cents, cost $2.00 and cover every emergency including both overdrafts. Blanket daily taps on the percent app at $144 against two of those emergency taps at $1.00 is a 144x spread on identical earnings.

Targeted cashout discipline is the same receipts-first pricing the site runs on every other hustle. The meter on the button bills a side income of $9,600 a year an extra $144 for a service the weekly payout delivers free. The rate matters more than the raw fee: at $22 an hour, $144 of fees is 6.5 hours of gig work spent pressing a button the bank does for free on Wednesdays. The metered button is a convenience line item, and it deserves the same treatment as any other line item: priced, compared to the free default, and pressed only when the emergency is real.
